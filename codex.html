Codex Matching Path

Codex lets researchers, scientists and citizen scientists work together to match animal sightings and collect detailed data about individual animals. I redesigned the page where that matching happens.

Overview

Codex combines structured wildlife research with artificial intelligence, citizen science and computer vision. Its goal is to speed up population analysis and find new insights that help fight extinction.

Why we started

Some users told us a competitor had better features and a nicer experience. We wanted to find out if that was true, and if we could make our product better. So we planned carefully from the start.

We followed the design thinking process. Along the way we faced real challenges: finding out what people actually wanted, handling very different opinions on how a feature should work, language barriers, and understanding the technical side of the product. We solved them one at a time.

We kept improving the designs until the result matched what competitors offered and went further. We listened closely to users, and the matching page became much easier to use.

Research goals

  1. Find out what problems users have when they try to match animals.
  2. Find out which features make the biggest difference to how users feel about matching.
  3. Make matching easier and more straightforward.

Research questions

  1. How do users use Wildbook day to day?
  2. What problems do users run into when matching individuals in Wildbook?
  3. How often do users skip steps because they are unclear or confusing?
  4. Which features do users ask for most, and why do they want them?
  5. How can we improve the matching flow so it is easier and faster?
  6. How do users see Wildbook compared with competing products like Honey Veli?

The problem

  • Matching animals was slow and tiring, and some matches took far too long.
  • Technical limits were one of the biggest problems users faced.
  • Many users didn't know which tools existed or how to use them.
  • A large share of users were older and less familiar with the product.
  • The interface was cluttered.

Where we started: the existing tools

Before designing anything, we studied how matching worked in the tools users already had: Flukebook and the 2022 version of Codex.

Understanding the user

User research, affinity mapping, personas

User research

We began by setting goals, defining the problem, and writing the questionnaire and script for our first round of user interviews.

The interviews threw us a curveball. People came from very different backgrounds, which was valuable but also hard. One person spoke very fast; another struggled with English. I adjusted as we went, and the interviews took longer than planned. Still, we learned a lot about how people use the product and what problems they face. Sorting through it all took time, but it gave us what we needed to design and test later.

I learned a lot about different people and their needs, and how important clear communication is. I now work on talking clearly with anyone, wherever they're from.

What we measured (KPIs)

  1. Time taken to match individuals.
  2. Skip rate: how often users skip steps because they're confusing.
  3. System Usability Scale (a standard usability score).
  4. User feedback.
  5. How complex users felt the design was.
  6. How easy the website was to use.
  7. How comfortable users felt while matching.

Starting the design

Wireframes, low fidelity, high fidelity, prototype, usability study

First ideas after research

Based on user needs, market trends and project goals, I sketched the first ideas for the match page. We had many directions, so we chose two versions to test in a usability study.

Design A wireframe
Design B wireframe

Helping users get started: onboarding

Many users had little experience with technology, and technical terms made the tool hard to understand. So we added onboarding screens to help people get started.

Flagging a match

Clicking the flag button opens a simple form to report a wrong match. This helps the team improve match accuracy, because users can tell us about problems as soon as they see them.

Helping users trust the results

Users needed to know how accurate a match was. By explaining the score, we help them trust the results and understand how precise they are.

"Why you should trust our score" tooltip wireframe

Showing what's selected

Clear highlights for selected items and obvious buttons help users move through the page and finish tasks faster.

Low fidelity

Low-fidelity designs showed us roughly how the final screens would look. There were many rounds of changes from the early wireframes. It took longer than expected, but after a lot of discussion we reached clear decisions.

High fidelity

Once we got the green light, we built high-fidelity mockups. They looked much better than the older designs (Wildbook and early QA Codex). I personally preferred Design B, but to stay objective we ran a usability study.

Usability study results

  • Users preferred Design B overall.
  • Onboarding was a draw, so we let product requirements decide.
  • Users liked some parts of each design.

As the project went on, technical limits and changing product requirements made us rethink parts of the final design.

Refining the design

Final high-fidelity mockup, accessibility

Final mockup

After research, usability testing and many rounds of changes, we reached the final match page.

We made some tough calls, like removing flagging and zoom controls for now. They aren't essential yet, but we see their value and plan to bring them back later.

  1. Inspect match area. Turning on this toggle highlights the part of the animal the algorithm used to match.
  2. View tutorial. Lets users see the onboarding screens again if they forget what a tool does.
  3. Image carousel. Shows different photos of the same animal.
  4. Flag removed. Useful, but lower on the priority list for now.
  5. Match status. Users set whether a match is pending, completed or undetermined.
  6. "No match" and "Confirm match". A simpler approach to "no match" saves space and reduces confusion by putting fewer options in the bottom bar.

Accessibility

To check: the old site had placeholder text here. I wrote these from what the final design shows. Edit them to match what you actually did.

  • Layout: the two photos sit side by side at the same size, so users can compare them without scrolling.
  • Navigation and components: clear selected states and labelled buttons, so users always know where they are and what a button does.
4

Moving forward

Takeaways, next steps

Impact

The usability studies gave a positive response to the new design. Users were interested in the new interface and how it could make their work easier.

What I learned

Research, talking to people and getting feedback taught me to see things from the user's side. We need to think about everyone who might use the product, and when there are barriers, find a way to remove them.

Next steps

As testing continues, I'm excited about what this could mean for the animals too. It's about making matching work for people, and also for animals whose photos are easier to match when stacked vertically.

In upcoming tests and brainstorming sessions, I want to keep improving the experience, both for the researchers using the tool and for the conservation work it supports.
